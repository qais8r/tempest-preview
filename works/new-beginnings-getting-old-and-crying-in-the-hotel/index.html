New Beginnings, Getting Old, & Crying in the Hotel

by Brett Chy

The week prior to my starting medical school, my parents and older brother joined me on my trek from Orange County, California to Phoenix, Arizona. We took two cars, each packed with boxes upon bags upon boxes of things that were meant to eventually fill my apartment. As with any trip involving my family, it was stressful, frustrating to no end, and exhausting as circles. Through our days of touring the campus, buying toiletries, and choosing between trashcan A and trashcan B, I made my fair share of snappy remarks and exasperated sighs towards none other than my parents, who so adamantly stretched out every conversation with their elaborate hypotheticals about what might go wrong if my bedside desk were made of metal instead of wood. Inevitably, and usually immediately, after each of my quips, I felt immense guilt for whatever I did or said, for being so dismissive, so bratty, and quite honestly, so rude toward my parents, both of whom I know did not have to be there for me, whom did not have to drive these six uncomfortable hours just to mull over Ikea dressers and carry countless boxes for me, whom did not have to sweat in this 105 degree heat for me. But who did, for me, because they wanted to. I wouldn’t say my frustration was unwarranted, considering the fountain of eternal stress my parents can sometimes become. But, I could’ve and should’ve been much kinder.

There was something new that came with this trip, as well–a problem of which I had noticed the faint outlines for many years, but never confronted so directly as I did in front of my new apartment, staring at my parents’ faces, almost shimmering from the heat around us. Suddenly, and in such high definition, I could see every muddy spot that stained the high points of their cheeks, layered upon each crepey line that pinched the corners of their eyes, shaped like small huts from the slack of their drooping. My parents are old, I thought to myself. No, no–my parents are just older than they used to be, I tried to reframe. I scanned them up and down carefully, as they rattled on about some worry that is now muted in this memory, superseded by the image of their two bodies side by side, bulging in some places, and tethered together by the innumerable trials they’ve shared in this life. I thought about everything they must’ve faced raising us, raising me, in this country they’ve been trying so hard to figure out–this world they are still working to map and mine through. This trip, I realized, was but the natural continuation of the lunches my mom had packed, the vacation days my dad had saved, the expenses and time all stashed away for a week like this. I couldn’t deny now, the marks of sacrifice that took residence in their skin, their hair, and even their voices, somehow, somewhere over these past few years. And yet, there they were–the two of them–more spotted, more worn than I ever imagined them becoming–still hoisting their pickaxes, still swinging their sore arms, still telling me to put down my suitcase to lighten my load. “Please, let me do it for you,” my dad said. When I refused his help, he maneuvered his hand to loosen my grip and took the handle into his own.

On the final day of our trip, the night before my parents and brother would drive back home to California, my brother and I decided to do some last minute shopping while my parents lounged in the hotel. With my brother at the wheel, we took the car which I would be driving here for the next four years, passed down to me from my sister. When we returned to the hotel and parked in the dim lot, my brother gave me a last minute review of all the functions of a 2020 Camry SE–what this button does, what this light means, what this lever adjusts–his final gift of knowledge on our last night together. And a reminder, too, of how little I knew about cars, I thought to myself, while sitting in the passenger seat that I had the luxury of occupying essentially my entire life. How little I knew about so many things.

The night was in full force, but still warm, nearing on hot, from the day’s lingering heat. I was still getting used to this. As we walked past the hotel doors and through its curated halls, I suddenly found myself trying hard to hold back tears. My brother stood facing the elevator, as did I. I was still as a tree. The analog panel counted down its red digits, and I felt myself, too, descending in some way. With the crescendo of whirring metal and in the thick suspense of an imminent ding, I forced myself a deep breath, trying my best to exhale as much fear and shame from my body as possible. In a voice crumbling with embarrassment, I turned to my brother and creaked, “Can we take a moment?” My words, caught in my throat, transformed spontatenously to tears. I was a child. We stood there for a while, the metal sliding door holding itself agape at the scene it was observing, though I’m sure it had seen it all in its lifetime. At the time, I couldn’t even identify the reason for my outburst. I couldn’t put a name to all the emotions I had felt over this past week–the feelings of guilt and regret and sadness and incompetence, muddied with the anxiety of this new beginning–starting medical school, having to make new friends, having to make these big choices about my small life. Everything in me collided, my head and my heart, their contents against one another. And it all came tumbling out in streams of tears and stifled cries, muffled by my brother’s arms.

He held me there for some time, helped calm me down to a state of comprehensibility. He asked me gently why I was feeling what I was feeling. And why else? And why else? And anything else? And what else do you want to talk about? And what else are you worried about? He asked insistently. I wondered if this was how he spoke to his patients. Or if maybe, the way he spoke to the kids he cared for was modeled, in part, from all his years of consoling me, his first patient, forever seven years behind him. We walked for a long while, circling the jigsaw-shaped building, talking about everything: our parents, our futures, growing up, getting old, becoming adults. Tears were shed, at points in pairs, drawn from the well of memories we shared. We were, of course, raised on the same, onerous love and tethered by the same, flightless fears. In the lulls of our conversation, the colors of this state seemed to fill our view. Muted browns and olive greens padded any moments of silence. The earth was terracotta in smell and hue. And we decided, ultimately, that it all came down to this: you are never a burden to the people you love. In fact, the burden of love is the greatest gift you can carry. That is to say: how grateful am I to carry this gift. Despite how confusing, how painful, how sometimes unbearable it may be, it is what keeps us together. It is what keeps us alive and well and willing–the only things, I continue realizing, that have ever mattered to me.

About the work

Moving to Arizona was a more emotional experience than I thought it would be. The feelings were immediate, of course, and the words came naturally over the next few days. Revisions occurred over the next few months.
